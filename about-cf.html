About Cystic Fibrosis

A single gene, a whole-body disease.

Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene, which normally directs the protein that moves salt and water across cell membranes. When CFTR doesn't work properly, mucus in the lungs, digestive tract, and other organs becomes thick and sticky, leading to chronic lung infections, poor nutrient absorption, and, if untreated, a shortened lifespan. That single change is what causes most of the challenges people with cystic fibrosis face day to day. It's one of the most common life-limiting genetic disorders worldwide, though its reported prevalence in African populations remains poorly characterized.

By the numbers

Worldwide, an estimated 45,000–50,000 children are born with cystic fibrosis each year, and tens of millions of people carry a single CFTR mutation without symptoms. In populations of European descent, where CF has been studied longest, incidence is roughly 1 in 2,500–3,500 live births, one of the most common life-limiting genetic disorders in those populations. What incidence looks like in Ghana and across West Africa is not yet known, because the newborn screening and genetic testing infrastructure needed to find out has never been built here. That absence of data is not evidence of absence of disease. It's the gap this foundation exists to bridge.

How CF affects the body

CF doesn't damage every organ directly. It changes the mucus, and it's that one change, repeated in different organs, that causes such different-looking problems:

  • Lungs. Normally, a thin layer of mucus traps dust and germs so tiny hair-like structures can sweep them out. In CF, the mucus is too thick to move, so it sits in the airways instead of clearing out. Germs get trapped along with it, which is why lung infections happen often and why chest physiotherapy, airway-clearance routines, and avoiding sick contacts become such a big part of daily life.
  • Pancreas. The pancreas makes enzymes that break down food so the body can absorb nutrients. Thick mucus can block the tiny ducts that carry these enzymes into the gut, so food passes through without being properly digested. This is one of the more serious and persistent challenges in CF care: the resulting malnutrition can lead to a pattern known as failure to thrive: poor weight gain and slow growth in children that, left unaddressed, can affect multiple organ systems. To compensate, many people with CF take pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) with every meal and snack, sometimes dozens of capsules a day, alongside close nutritional monitoring.
  • Liver. The liver produces bile to help digest fats. If mucus blocks the bile duct, bile backs up instead of flowing normally, and over years this can scar the liver. Not everyone with CF develops liver problems, but regular monitoring helps catch it early.
  • Reproductive system. In most men with CF, the tube that carries sperm (the vas deferens) is blocked or missing from birth, which usually causes infertility, though testicular function itself is typically unaffected, so options like assisted reproduction can still make biological fatherhood possible. Women with CF can also face fertility challenges, often linked to thicker cervical mucus.
  • Joints. Less widely known, CF can also affect the joints. CF-related arthritis causes episodes of joint pain, swelling, and stiffness, often in the knees, ankles, and wrists, separate from any lung infection. It can flare up on its own, be mistaken for other conditions, and is still frequently under-recognized even by clinicians familiar with CF.

Signs to watch for

CF doesn't always announce itself

No single sign confirms CF on its own, but this combination, especially in a child who isn't thriving, is worth raising with a clinician.

Salty-tasting skinNoticeably salty sweat, often first noticed when a child is kissed.

Persistent coughA chronic, often wet cough that doesn't resolve with standard treatment.

Recurrent lung infectionsRepeated pneumonia or bronchitis, sometimes misread as tuberculosis.

Poor weight gainDifficulty gaining weight or growing despite a normal appetite.

Greasy, bulky stoolsA sign that fat isn't being digested properly due to pancreatic involvement.

Frequent sinus issuesChronic sinusitis or nasal polyps, especially in older children and adults.

Getting a diagnosis

The pathway from suspicion to confirmed diagnosis

1

Clinical suspicion

A clinician notices a pattern, such as recurrent chest infections, poor growth, or digestive symptoms, that doesn't fit the usual explanations.

2

Sweat chloride test

A small, painless test measures the salt content of sweat. Elevated chloride levels are the frontline indicator of CF.

3

Genetic confirmation

CFTR gene sequencing identifies the specific mutations involved, confirming the diagnosis and classifying it by mutation class.

4

Care team formation

A pulmonologist, dietitian, and physiotherapist, ideally connected through a CF-aware clinic, begin coordinated, ongoing care.

The CFTR gene

Six classes of mutation

Not all CFTR mutations behave the same way. Clinicians group them into six functional classes, and where a person's mutation falls shapes both their symptoms and their treatment options.

I

No protein made

A premature stop signal means the CFTR protein is never produced at all.

II

Protein misfolded

CFTR is made but folded incorrectly and degraded before it reaches the cell surface: the class behind the most common CF mutation worldwide, F508del.

III

Gating defect

CFTR reaches the surface but its channel doesn't open properly to let chloride through.

IV

Reduced conductance

The channel opens, but chloride flow through it is diminished.

V

Reduced quantity

Less CFTR protein is produced than normal, lowering overall function.

VI

Reduced stability

CFTR reaches the surface and functions, but breaks down faster than it should.

The Africa gap

Why CF in Ghana is harder to find and harder to treat

Missing from the databases

Reference resources like CFTR2 and ClinVar were built largely from European and North American cohorts. African CFTR variants are dramatically underrepresented, which makes interpreting a Ghanaian patient's genetic result harder than it should be.

Missed in the clinic

Without local variant data or routine newborn screening, CF is frequently mistaken for tuberculosis, pneumonia, or malnutrition, and diagnosed late, if at all.

Out of reach once found

Even after diagnosis, CFTR modulator therapies that target the underlying protein defect remain largely unavailable and unaffordable across sub-Saharan Africa.

The sweat test. Long before genetics, caregivers across cultures noticed that babies with CF tasted unusually salty when kissed, because faulty CFTR lets extra salt leak into sweat. That observation is still the basis of the sweat chloride test, the frontline diagnostic tool for CF today, ideally followed by genetic testing to confirm and classify the mutation.

Common questions

What people usually ask first

  • Is CF contagious? No. CF is genetic, not infectious. It can't be caught or spread from person to person.
  • Is there a cure? Not yet, but treatment has advanced significantly. Newer drugs called CFTR modulators target the underlying protein defect rather than just its symptoms, and for the mutations they cover, they've meaningfully improved both life expectancy and quality of life.
  • Can someone with CF live a normal life? With consistent care, yes. Many people with CF attend school, work, have relationships and families, and are living well into adulthood in places where care is accessible. Ghana's gap is largely about access to that care, not about the disease itself being unmanageable.
  • Do both parents have to carry the gene? Yes. CF is recessive, meaning a child needs to inherit one faulty CFTR gene from each parent to have the condition. Carriers (with just one copy) are healthy and usually don't know they carry it.
